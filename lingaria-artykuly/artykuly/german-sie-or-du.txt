English lets you say "you" to a toddler and to a bank manager. German makes you choose every time. Pick *du* and you are speaking to a friend, a child or a classmate. Pick *Sie* and you are speaking to a stranger, a customer or someone senior. Choosing the wrong one rarely causes a scene, but it changes the tone of the conversation.

## What changes

The pronoun is only part of it. The verb changes too, and so does the word for "you" after the verb:

- *du kommst*, *Wie geht es dir?*: informal, to one person;
- *Sie kommen*, *Wie geht es Ihnen?*: formal, to one person or to many;
- *ihr kommt*: informal, to a group of people you would call *du* one by one.

*Sie* always has a capital S, even in the middle of a sentence, which is how you tell it from *sie*, "she" or "they". Its verb form is the same as the one for "they": *sie kommen* and *Sie kommen* look identical apart from the capital. *Sie* also has no plural of its own. It is used for one person and for ten, so *Können Sie mir helfen?* works for a single shop assistant or for a whole counter of them.

:uwaga
The plural of *du* is *ihr*, and that one is easy to forget. With a group of friends you say *Kommt ihr mit?* ("Are you coming along?"). With a group of strangers you stay with *Kommen Sie mit?*. The verb ending changes with it: *du kommst*, but *ihr kommt*.
:koniec

## Who gets du and who gets Sie

You say *du* to family, friends, children and pets, to fellow students, to teammates in a sports club, and to most people your age at a party or in a bar. Online communities, apps and ads aimed at younger people also use *du*. IKEA's German advertising and website address customers with *du*, for example, although in the store itself you may still be addressed with *Sie*.

You say *Sie* to anyone you meet in a professional or service role: a shop assistant, a doctor, an official at a counter, a hotel receptionist, a customer on the phone. The same goes for adults you do not know, especially if they are clearly older than you. In writing, a formal email or letter opens with *Sehr geehrte Frau Schmidt* (Dear Ms Schmidt) or *Sehr geehrter Herr Müller*.

The safe default for a learner is *Sie* with every adult stranger, until they use *du* first. Being too polite costs you very little. Being too casual with the wrong person can sound cheeky.

## How someone offers you du

You do not decide to switch by yourself. One person offers, and the other agrees. Traditional etiquette (the Knigge guidelines) says the offer goes from top to bottom: the older person to the younger one, the boss to an employee, and between colleagues of equal rank the one who has been there longer. Today this is a guideline rather than a strict rule, but it explains why you should wait.

The usual offers sound like this:

- *Wollen wir uns duzen?* ("Shall we use *du*?");
- *Wir können uns gern duzen.* ("We can use *du* if you like.");
- *Sagen Sie ruhig du zu mir.* ("Feel free to say *du* to me.").

After that you are *per du*, a set phrase for being on *du* terms. The opposite is *per Sie*. The verbs are *duzen* (to use *du*) and *siezen* (to use *Sie*). There is even an old custom for it, *Brüderschaft trinken*: two people seal the *du* friendship with a drink, traditionally with their arms linked.

If someone offers *du* and you would rather not, you can decline politely: *Ich würde lieber beim Sie bleiben.* ("I'd rather stay with *Sie*.") Said with a smile, it is a normal sentence and not an insult.

## Sie or du at work

Practice varies a lot from one workplace to the next. In many start-ups, tech companies and creative offices everyone says *du*, often all the way up to the management. In banks, law firms, public offices and traditional companies, *Sie* is still common. Some places mix the two: colleagues are *du*, but the boss or a client is *Sie*.

The easiest way to find out is to listen for the first week. Hear how people greet each other in the corridor, check how emails open (*Hallo Anna,* or *Sehr geehrter Herr Müller,*) and copy that. A new employee says *Sie* until someone says otherwise, and in a *du* office, answering a *du* offer with *Sie* would feel odd.

Two hybrid forms are worth recognizing, even though you will not need to produce them. They have nicknames: the *Hamburger Sie* and the *Münchner Du*. The first is the first name plus *Sie* (*Anna, können Sie bitte kurz kommen?*). The second is the surname plus *du* (*Müller, kommst du mal?*). If you hear one of them, nobody has made a mistake.

## Children and teenagers

Adults say *du* to children without thinking about it. Somewhere in the teens the line gets blurry. There is no official age at which *du* turns into *Sie*, and a teenager in a shop may hear either one, depending on the speaker.

## If you get it wrong

Say *du* to someone who expected *Sie*, and they may answer with *Sie*, a polite hint. Switch back at once. A short *Entschuldigung* is enough. People rarely take offence at a learner, who is assumed to be still learning.

Say *Sie* to a friend or a younger colleague and nothing bad happens. You sound distant, and you may hear *Wir können uns gern duzen* in return. Say yes.
