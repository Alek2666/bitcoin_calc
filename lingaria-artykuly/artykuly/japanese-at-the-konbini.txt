A Japanese convenience store checkout is a quick quiz in polite Japanese, and you can pass it with two answers. The clerk asks about a bag, about heating your food, about chopsticks and a point card. You only need to recognize them and reply with *onegaishimasu* (yes, please) or *daijōbu desu* (no, I'm fine).

## Walking in

The first thing you hear is いらっしゃいませ (*irasshaimase*), "welcome". Clerks say it to every customer who comes through the door, often without looking up. It is not a question and nobody expects a reply. A small nod is plenty.

Japanese has pitch, not stress, so the pronunciations here use no capital letters. A macron (*ō*, *ū*) marks a long vowel, held about twice as long. A doubled consonant, as in *irasshaimase*, is a tiny pause before the second one. The final *u* in *desu* and *masu* is nearly silent, so they come out as "dess" and "mahss".

## The bag question

Since 1 July 2020, shops in Japan have been required to charge for plastic shopping bags, convenience stores included. Shops set their own price, and the big convenience chains began at around 3 yen, which is why most checkouts now include a bag question. The usual wording is 袋はご利用ですか (*fukuro wa goriyō desu ka*), literally "will you use a bag?". Some clerks say 袋にお入れしますか (*fukuro ni oire shimasu ka*), "shall I put it in a bag?". The plainer 袋は要りますか (*fukuro wa irimasu ka*) means the same, but staff tend to use the politer forms.

If you buy a single item, such as a drink, the clerk may offer a sticker on it instead of a bag: シールでよろしいでしょうか (*shīru de yoroshii deshō ka*). You can answer はい、大丈夫です (*hai, daijōbu desu*) and carry on.

:uwaga
大丈夫です (*daijōbu desu*) literally means "it's all right", and that is the trap. To "will you use a bag?" it means "no, thanks". To "is a sticker okay?" the same words mean "yes, that's fine". The question decides the meaning, so listen to it, and add a small shake of the head or a flat hand wave when you mean no. A clearer no is 要りません (*irimasen*), "I don't need it", though it sounds a little blunt. 結構です (*kekkō desu*) is also common, but it is just as ambiguous.
:koniec

## Heating, chopsticks and the point card

Buy a bento or another food that goes in the microwave and you will hear 温めますか (*atatamemasu ka*), "shall I heat it?". Answer お願いします for yes or 大丈夫です for no.

Next comes お箸はお付けしますか (*ohashi wa otsuke shimasu ka*), "would you like chopsticks?". The same pattern works for a spoon (スプーン, *supūn*) or a fork (フォーク, *fōku*). To say yes, ask for the item directly: お箸をお願いします (*ohashi o onegaishimasu*).

Finally, ポイントカードはお持ちですか (*pointo kādo wa omochi desu ka*). Lawson uses Ponta, for example, and visitors usually have no card. The answer is 持っていません (*motte imasen*), "I don't have one".

:cta

## Paying

Cash goes on the small tray beside the register (カルトン, *karuton*) rather than into the clerk's hand. The clerk may ask お支払いはどうなさいますか (*oshiharai wa dō nasaimasu ka*), "how would you like to pay?". To pay by card, answer カードでお願いします (*kādo de onegaishimasu*) and then tap or insert the card at the reader; for cash, 現金で (*genkin de*) replaces カードで. レシートはご利用ですか (*reshīto wa goriyō desu ka*) is about the ordinary till receipt. A formal one for expense claims is a 領収書 (*ryōshūsho*), and you ask for it by name: 領収書をお願いします (*ryōshūsho o onegaishimasu*).

Buying beer or cigarettes adds a step. A screen at the register asks you to confirm that you are 20 or older, and the clerk says something like 年齢確認のボタンを押してください (*nenrei kakunin no botan o oshite kudasai*), "please press the age-confirmation button". The legal age for alcohol and tobacco in Japan is still 20, even though adulthood starts at 18. Pressing the button is normally how you confirm it.

## Hot snacks

Fried chicken and steamed buns sit behind the counter, not on the shelves. Say the name or point and say これ (*kore*), "this one", then add *hitotsu* (one) or *futatsu* (two) and *kudasai*.
